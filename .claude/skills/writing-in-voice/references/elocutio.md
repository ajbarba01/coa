# elocutio

How the text is worded. Every entry gives a check, why the failure reads as
machine-written, and a worked pair.

The last three entries are overcorrections: a rewrite installs them rather
than removing them. Check those against the draft, not against the source.

## Checks

### Vary sentence length

**Check:** No three consecutive sentences land within two words of each other
in length. The one exemption is three short sentences under ten words
deployed for a deliberate three-beat cadence.

**The bound:** Manufactured punchlines. Satisfying this check by dropping in
a two-word verdict trades a flat rhythm for a louder one. Vary length by
letting each thought take the room it needs, not by inserting a sentence
whose only job is to be short.

**Why:** Sentence length in human prose tracks attention. A writer drops
short when they want you to stop and runs long when they're working
something out in front of you, so when every sentence comes out the same
size the reader loses the only signal prose has for what matters. The
paragraph ends up perfectly clear and impossible to skim: nothing in it is
raised and nothing is thrown away.

**Before:** Our platform provides a range of tools that help teams manage
their work more effectively. Users can create projects, assign tasks, and
monitor progress from a single dashboard. The system also offers reporting
features that give managers visibility into team performance.
**After:** Teams plan work here. Projects and tasks sit on one dashboard,
sorted by what's late, so a manager who opens it on Monday morning can see
within a few seconds which people are blocked and who they are waiting on
before deciding whether any of it is worth a meeting. The report is one page.

### Make the verb do the work

**Check:** Two directions, same failure. Put "is" or "has" back where an
elaborate verb stands in for it — serves as, stands as, represents, marks,
boasts, features, offers. And collapse a verb phrase into the verb hiding
inside it: "made a decision" is "decided," "has the ability to" is "can,"
"provides support for" is "supports."

**Why:** "Is" makes a flat claim that nothing decorates, and a sentence built
on it can only be interesting if the fact is. The substitutes sound more
considered while asserting the same thing, and several assert slightly less —
"represents" and "serves as" soften identity into resemblance. A nominalized
verb does the reverse damage: it buries the action in a noun and needs a
weak verb to carry it, so the sentence gets longer while the thing that
happened gets vaguer.

**Before:** The lobby serves as the staging area for a game and provides
support for the sharing of a join code.
**After:** The lobby is where a game gets staged. It has a join code you can
share.

---

### Active voice, human subjects

**Check:** The thing doing the verb can do it. "The decision emerged," "the
data suggests," "concerns were raised" — name who decided, who suggests, who
raised.

**Why:** An agentless passive is what gets produced when the actor is
unknown, unflattering, or was never checked, and it survives review because
no one objects to a sentence that blames nobody. Inanimate subjects doing
human verbs are the same evasion with more confidence: a decision cannot
emerge, and writing that it did quietly removes the person who made it from
the account.

The legitimate passive is the one where the actor is genuinely unimportant or
genuinely unknown — "the file was deleted at 04:12" is fine when nobody knows
who deleted it, and better than inventing an actor to satisfy a rule.

**Before:** A decision was made to deprecate the endpoint, and concerns were
raised about the migration timeline.
**After:** We're deprecating the endpoint in March. Two teams have said the
timeline is too tight.

---

### Cut words that don't change meaning

**Check:** Delete each word in turn; if the sentence still means what it
meant, the deletion stands. Test especially "simply," "just," "actually,"
"truly," "literally," "please note that," "in order to," "the process of,"
and openers like "it's worth noting that."

**The carve-out:** courtesy that lands on bad news. When the message refuses
the reader, blocks them, or asks them to go out of their way after something
went wrong, a "please" or a "sorry" is the only thing marking that a person
is on the other end of a system that just said no. The word to cut on a happy
path is the word to keep on a blocked one.

**Why:** Filler feels like politeness while you're writing it, which is
exactly why it survives editing — cutting "simply click the link" to "click
the link" feels like removing warmth. The reader isn't measuring warmth.
They're measuring how many words stand between them and the instruction.

**Before:** In order to reset your password, please simply click the link
below, and the process of verification will begin automatically.
**After:** Click the link below to reset your password. Verification starts
automatically.

---

### Fact stacking

**Check:** No noun phrase carries a modifier that introduces a new fact. One
fact per clause. A fact that needs saying gets a clause of its own or waits
for the section built for it.

**Why:** This is what a fact sheet does to a sentence when the writer is
trying to discharge all of it at once. Each new fact gets stapled to whatever
noun is nearest, so the sentence never returns to its main clause and just
accretes — stands *with the tools to use them*, mechanics *on the floor
during opening hours* *who will answer whatever you ask* *and will not touch
your bike*. Nothing there is padding. Every modifier is true, which is what
makes the failure hard to see from inside the draft: the writer is hitting a
quota, not decorating.

**Not the same as Cut words that don't change meaning.** That check removes
words carrying nothing. This one relocates words carrying something.

**The bounds:**

- *A clause is the unit, not a sentence.* Two facts joined by a connective
  are two clauses and satisfy this check. Name the relation still applies,
  and splitting a related pair into unjoined sentences to satisfy this one is
  the wrong repair.
- *Vary sentence length still applies.* Facts broken out into a row of short
  same-shaped clauses is Manufactured punchlines wearing a different hat.
- *This does not soften inventio's Prefer the specific fact over the
  abstraction.* That check replaces a vague noun with a precise one. This one
  is about hanging an extra fact off a noun that was already precise.

**Before:** There are eight repair stands with the tools to use them, and two
mechanics on the floor during opening hours who will answer whatever you ask
them and will not touch your bike.
**After:** There are eight repair stands and two mechanics who can answer any
of your questions.

### Name the relation

**Check:** Where two adjacent sentences stand in a relation — contrast, gloss,
consequence, condition — a connective names it. Unmarked adjacency is correct
only where the sentences are genuinely independent.

**Why:** Sentences produced one at a time arrive self-contained, so a
paragraph assembled out of them is a set of observations rather than one idea
being developed. The reader registers it as a seam, and the usual repair is
worse than the fault: a writer who can hear the gap fills it with drama — a
clipped verdict, an invented aside — where a two-letter conjunction was what
the sentence wanted. The cause is often a length cap. A thought that wants
subordination gets chopped into two coordinate sentences, which strips the
back-reference and shortens both halves at once.

Restating a figure in friendlier units is fine, so long as the sentence says
that's what it's doing. "61%, meaning six in ten" is a gloss. The same two
numbers in consecutive unmarked sentences read as two findings.

**Before:** Roughly one order in twelve arrives after the date we quoted.
That is about eighty parcels a week.
**After:** Roughly one order in twelve arrives after the date we quoted,
meaning about eighty parcels a week.

---

### Conjunctive overload

**Check:** Delete a "moreover." If the paragraph is unchanged, the connective
was never carrying meaning. Furthermore, additionally, in addition, that
said.

**Why:** Discourse markers are cheap coherence. They assert that the next
sentence follows from the last without requiring it to, and a reader who
follows two or three and finds no consequence waiting stops believing the
connectives.

This is Name the relation inverted, and the two are filed together because
each is the other's overcorrection. That entry covers tissue missing where a
relation is real; this one covers tissue asserting a relation that was never
there. A single "however" is punctuation between thoughts — this failure is
about accumulation.

**Before:** The editor supports keyboard shortcuts. Furthermore, it saves
your work automatically. Additionally, version history is available for
thirty days.
**After:** The editor saves as you type and keeps thirty days of history.
Press ? to see the shortcuts.

---

### Elegant variation

**Check:** Swap every variant back to a single word, and if nothing is lost,
the variation was decoration paid for out of the reader's attention. A match
becomes a round, then a contest, then a bout, with no distinction intended.

**Why:** Repeating a noun is invisible to a reader; replacing it is not.
Every synonym forces a decision about whether something new has entered the
paragraph, and the reader who guesses wrong has to reread. The habit comes
from treating repetition as a flaw rather than as the thing that holds a
paragraph to one subject.

**Before:** Each match runs for ninety seconds. The round ends once every
player has answered, and the contest is scored when the bout closes.
**After:** Each match runs for ninety seconds and is scored once every player
has answered.

### Overexplanation

**Check:** Cover the second clause and ask whether the reader would still do
the right thing. If they would, the first clause was the whole message.

**The bound:** `dispositio.md`'s Say what happens next. Naming a next step the
reader would otherwise have to hunt for is that rule; naming one that is on
screen is this failure.

**Why:** An instruction is finished once the reader can act on it, and that
point arrives earlier than it feels like it should while writing. The extra
clause is cheap because a reason can be generated for any instruction at all,
and what it costs is the instruction's edge — a reader who meets a long
sentence starts working out which half they need, and on a failure message
that hesitation lands when they have least patience for it.

**Before:** Not all teams are ready. Use Start anyway to override.
**After:** Not all teams are ready.

---

### Dressed instructions

**Check:** An imperative stays an imperative. The correct move is to stop.
You'll want to run the migration first. The recommended approach is to batch
them.

**Why:** An imperative commits the writer to the advice; an evaluation
reports on it from a step back, which sounds considered rather than bossy and
nobody objects to it in review. The reader has to convert the sentence into
an action before using it, which they do without noticing on the first one
and resent by the fourth. The retrospective form is the worst of the family,
as it names what someone should have done rather than what to do now.

**Before:** If the last paragraph is the first paragraph with synonyms, the
page ran out of things to say a paragraph ago and should have stopped there.
**After:** If the last paragraph is the first paragraph with synonyms, delete
it.

---

### Superficial `-ing` clauses

**Check:** A trailing participial phrase earns its place by adding a fact.
Helping you stay organized. Ensuring a smooth process. Highlighting the
team's commitment.

**Why:** The clause answers a question the reader didn't ask, and answers it
by restating the main clause in warmer words. It's grammatically subordinate
and semantically empty, which makes it the safest thing to generate when a
sentence feels too short.

**Before:** Reminders sync across all your devices, ensuring you never miss
an important deadline.
**After:** Reminders sync across your devices. Dismiss one on your phone and
it's gone from your laptop too.

---

### Rule of threes

**Check:** Hold the third term against the other two. If it names no property
they left out, it was there for the rhythm.

**Why:** Three is the cadence of finished-sounding prose. Once "fast, secure,
and" is on the page a third term arrives whether or not a third property is
true, and it's often a synonym of the first.

**Before:** A fast, secure, and reliable way to share files with your team.
**After:** Send files up to 5 GB. Links expire when you tell them to.

---

### Colon reveals

**Check:** A colon introduces a list, a label, or a quotation. A noun phrase
followed by a colon and a dramatic lowercase reveal is a sentence wearing a
costume.

**Why:** The shape borrows the timing of a punchline without the material for
one, and it can be generated from any fact whatsoever, which is what makes it
cheap. Two in a page and every claim starts sounding like the last one was
holding out on you.

**Before:** The detail that makes it work: a separate agent grades the
output.
**After:** A separate agent grades the output, which is what makes it work.

---

### Em-dash and semicolon overuse

**Check:** At most one em dash per thousand words, and never two in a
paragraph. Count them before returning the draft — the proof that the rest
were disposable is that each one could have been a period.

**Why:** The em dash is the default resolution for a clause the writer hasn't
decided how to subordinate, and appositive afterthoughts are cheap to produce
in bulk. Neither mark is the problem — the frequency is. What accumulates is
a rhythm of continual qualification, where no statement finishes without an
amendment.

**Before:** Our pricing is simple — three tiers, no hidden fees — and you can
switch at any time; downgrades take effect at the end of the cycle — no
questions asked.
**After:** Three tiers, no hidden fees. Switch whenever you want. Downgrades
take effect at the end of the billing cycle.

---

### Match tense and person to the surface

**Check:** One point of view and one tense across a block of text, fragments
included. If the surface is second person present, a fragment reads as an
implied "you," not an implied "the user."

**Why:** A surface settles into a point of view before any single sentence
gets written, and the job of any one sentence is to stay inside what was
decided rather than relitigate it. Switching from "you can filter the list"
to "users who need more detail should click a row" asks the reader to work
out mid-sentence whether the second sentence is still about them.

**Before:** You can filter the list by status. Users who need more detail
should click a row to expand it.
**After:** You can filter the list by status. Click a row to see more detail.

---

### Write the fragment if the fragment is clearer

**Check:** A fragment stays if the full sentence would add only a subject and
a verb the reader doesn't need. It goes if the missing words carry
information — "Failed." omits the one thing the reader needed.

**Why:** "No files yet" is a complete thought even though it isn't a complete
sentence, and supplying "you have not added any files yet" restores grammar
nobody was missing. Fragments read naturally wherever the missing words are
obvious from context: a label, a status, an empty state.

**Before:** You have not added any files yet.
**After:** No files yet.

---

### The double edge

**Check:** Where one cause is presented as producing exactly one virtue and
one flaw, check whether the two effects sit on the same axis. Where one is
about how the text lands and the other about whether it's true, they were
never a pair.

**Why:** The shape manufactures the feeling of insight out of a single
observation: you notice one thing, split it into a good half and a bad half
so the sentence has somewhere to land, and the symmetry does the work an
argument would otherwise have to do. It survives its own repair — cut the
balanced clause and the move reappears a sentence later as two parallel
sentences, often sharing an opening.

**Before:** That gap is the source of both its authority and its emptiness.
**After:** No particular case is on the hook, so no particular case can
refute it, and a sentence nothing can refute is easy to mistake for one that
got something right.

### Manufactured punchlines

**Check:** Two shapes, and one instance of either is enough. A run of short
declaratives — count consecutive sentences under about eight words, and past
two ask what the third is for. And the lone verdict fragment: a short
sentence whose only content is the conclusion of the sentence before it.
"Twenty can." "Nothing to interpret." "That's the whole mechanism."

**Why:** A short sentence gets its force from the sentences around it, so
five in a row leaves nothing for any of them to be short against and the
emphasis cancels itself out. What survives is tone without event. The lone
verdict is the same claim made in one move rather than five: brevity
asserting weight that the content never supplied, which is Inflated
significance operating on a sentence instead of a fact.

This is the overcorrection paired with Vary sentence length. A writer told to
vary cadence discovers that clipped sentences feel decisive, and the cheapest
way to satisfy a variance rule is to drop a two-word verdict in — so the
verdict fragment is manufactured by the check that sits above it, and the
repair is to cut it rather than to replace it with a longer verdict.

**Before:** Then the timer hit zero. No more guesses. No second chances. The
board was locked.
**After:** When the timer hits zero the board locks, and anything still
sitting in the answer box is discarded.

---

### Aphorism formulas

**Check:** Ask what would have to be true for the sentence to be false. If
nothing would, replace it with the concrete claim it was standing in for. X
is the Y of Z. The currency of. The architecture of.

**Delete, don't improve.** A kicker rewritten into a better metaphor is still
a kicker. Cut it and end on the clearest concrete sentence already there.

**Why:** A maxim covers more ground than the writer checked. No particular
case is on the hook, so no particular case can refute it, and a sentence
nothing can refute is easy to mistake for one that got something right. This
shape drops into any paragraph that needs an ending.

**Before:** In a party game, attention is the real currency.
**After:** Players waiting more than about twenty seconds for a turn start
checking their phones, and they answer slower when it comes.

---

### Conversational rhetorical openers

**Check:** A staged pause before an ordinary statement. Honestly? Look.
Here's the thing. Let's be honest. Also the self-answered pair: a question,
then the answer the writer had all along.

**Why:** The move implies the writer is about to step off the official line,
and then the thing they say is the official line. Candour is signalled rather
than spent, and a person actually being frank says the frank thing, because
the whole value of frankness is that it arrives without preparation. This is
the overcorrection paired with `dispositio.md`'s Empty openers: a writer told
to stop opening on generalities reaches for fake intimacy instead.

**Before:** Is tournament mode worth using? Honestly? It depends on how many
teams you have.
**After:** Tournament mode earns its keep above about six teams. Below that
the round-robin finishes fast enough that projected standings never tell you
anything the real ones won't.

## Vocabulary

These words have no entry of their own. A hit gets reported under whichever
check it was doing work for, which is often an inventio one. Grep them when a
first pass turns up nothing and the text still reads wrong.

> align with, beacon, crucial, cutting-edge, delve, elevate, embark,
> emphasizing, empower, enduring, enhance, ever-evolving, facilitate, foster,
> game changer, garner, harness, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate, key
> (adjective), landscape (figurative), leverage, meticulous, multifaceted,
> paradigm shift, paramount, pivotal, realm, robust, seamless, showcase,
> streamline, supercharge, tapestry, testament, transformative, underscore,
> utilize, vibrant

One of these is evidence of nothing. Two in a paragraph, next to a triplet,
is a confession.

## What not to flag

- **Polish.** Clean grammar and consistent style mean the writer is good or
  was edited.
- **Mixed registers.** Casual and formal in one paragraph usually means a
  technical writer, or someone whose prose habits run that way.
- **Dryness.** Flat writing without any of the failures above is flat
  writing. These entries describe specific habits, not an absence of charm.
- **Hard vocabulary.** The list above is overused; "ostensibly" and
  "constituent" are not. Don't flatten a word for sounding educated.
- **One em dash.** Voice. The entry is explicit that frequency is the tell.
- **One short sentence.** Emphasis. Manufactured punchlines needs a run.
- **"Honestly" or "look" mid-sentence.** Ordinary. The tell is the standalone
  theatrical opener.
- **A passive with no useful actor.** "The file was deleted at 04:12" beats
  inventing someone to have deleted it.

## Signs to preserve

- **Dated references.** Slang and in-jokes that pin to a year and a
  subculture.
- **A defensible choice.** An odd word or an abrupt cut the writer could
  explain if asked.
- **Spoken rhythm.** "I think," "maybe," a digression that carries character.
  These read as hedges to a checklist and as a person to a reader.

## Profile

Derived from shipped desktop-application interface copy — VS Code (structured
localization file, the spine), Spotify, Notion, Slack, GitHub Desktop, Docker
Desktop — 2026-07-31, 38,671 strings / ~233,000 words after filtering to English
user-facing text.

### Capitalization is a function of element kind

The corpus does not pick one case and apply it everywhere. It picks by what the
element *is*, and applies that without exception.

- (observed) **Every string starts with a capital letter.** Command labels 100%
  in the spine (98% across all sources), other labels 81–84%, sentences 96%. A
  lowercase opening is 3% of the spine and reads as an unstyled fragment.
- (observed) **Commands and actions take Title Case** — 66% in the spine, 51%
  across all sources. "Terminate Task", "Remove from Recently Opened", "Check for
  Updates". The residue below 100% is almost entirely multi-word commands keeping
  articles and prepositions lowercase, which is Title Case, not an exception.
- (observed) **Labels that name a state or a thing take sentence case** — Title
  Case only 33% in the spine, 16% across all sources. "No snippet available",
  "Background Activities" split along exactly this line: the first reports, the
  second names a region.
- (observed) **Prose takes sentence case and a terminal period**, 96% both.

### Punctuation and register

- (observed) **The em dash does not appear. 0.00 per 1000 words across 233,000
  words.** Not rare — absent. Where a qualifier is needed it goes in parentheses
  (7.8 per 1000) or a second sentence.
- (observed) Semicolons are 0.46–0.50 per 1000 words; exclamation marks 0.17 in
  the spine. Both are effectively reserved.
- (observed) Contractions appear in 0–1% of strings. The register is uncontracted
  without being stiff: "does not", "cannot", "will not".
- (observed) Sentence length clusters tightly: median 10 words, p10 5, p90 24 —
  **overrides the baseline "vary sentence length" check.** That check governs
  consecutive prose; interface strings are independent and each is read alone, so
  a corpus-wide clustering around one length is correct here rather than flat.
- (inferred) No string in the sample used any word from the `## Vocabulary` list.
  Inferred rather than observed because it is an absence over a filtered corpus.
